# Resume Writing Guidelines

Principles for writing a strong technical CV using this template — and in general.

---

## Core Philosophy

**Your CV is optimised for scan speed.**

That is the correct philosophy. A good CV is not supposed to tell your whole life story. It is supposed to make someone think, very fast, *"this person is real, technical, useful, and worth interviewing."*

---

## Bullet Writing

- **Keep bullets tight.** Most bullets should be one line, two at most. Recruiters scan on first pass — they do not read deeply.
- **One bullet = one idea.** Don't stuff five unrelated claims into one sentence. Each bullet should carry a single contribution or capability.
- **Lead with action verbs.** *Built, Integrated, Created, Reviewed, Refactored, Led, Engineered, Owned.* This signals agency, not passive participation.
- **Show scope fast.** Reveal the size or shape of the work early — e.g. *"2 production backend services," "46+ PRs," "100+ teams."* This helps the reader calibrate importance immediately.
- **Embed the tech stack directly into the bullet.** Show tools in context rather than listing them separately. That is stronger than keyword dumping.
- **Follow the structure: what → how → why.** What you did, how you did it, why it mattered. That pattern is what makes technical CV bullets readable.
- **Avoid first-person language.** Drop "I did this" and "I was responsible for." Keep it sharp and professional.
- **Avoid paragraph blocks.** Dense prose kills recruiter attention.

---

## Structure and Ordering

- **Reverse-chronological ordering.** Newest and most relevant work goes first. That is how people actually evaluate CVs.
- **Strongest material near the top.** Experience first, then education, then projects — correct ordering for someone with real work and technical project signal.
- **Sectioning should be clean and recruiter-friendly.** Experience, Education, Projects, Certifications, Awards, Publications, Volunteer, Referees. No weird experimental structure.
- **Compress the contact header into one line.** Name, email, LinkedIn, GitHub, blog, phone. Good use of top-of-page real estate.

---

## Dates and Timeline

- **Dates must be visible and consistent.** Every major item should have a clear time range aligned to the right. Timeline clarity reduces recruiter friction.

---

## Describing Work

- **Achievement-driven, not responsibility-driven.** Describe what you changed or built, not just your job description.
- **Projects should read like real engineering work.** Frame them in terms of architecture, infra, CI/CD, SDKs, auth, RAG, pipelines — not student fluff.
- **Impact through outcomes, not buzzwords.** *Catching regressions early, improving maintainability, faster debugging, reliable automation* — that is better than *"worked on AI systems."*
- **Volunteer work counts.** Treat it like real work when it's relevant. It expands your signal without looking like filler.

---

## Content and Keywords

- **Decent keyword density for ATS without becoming unreadable.** Include relevant tools and technologies (e.g. FastAPI, RabbitMQ, GitHub Actions, AWS Lambda, DynamoDB, OAuth, JWT, RAG, Cloudflare Workers) — but keep the document readable as prose.
- **Compress breadth without sacrificing specificity.** That balance is hard to get right; aim for it.

---

## Specific Sections

- **Education:** Compress rather than overexplain. Don't waste space describing obvious things.
- **Awards:** Title, rank/result, and a short explanation. No long celebratory prose.
- **Publications:** Label accurately — e.g. *"manuscript in preparation"* instead of implying publication. That is the honest and correct move.
- **Links:** Include GitHub, blog, and LinkedIn to support your claims, but the document should still be self-contained.

---

## What to Avoid

- Separating skills from experience into a detached keyword block.
- Aesthetic gimmicks. A CV is a sales document for competence, not a design poster.
- Over-explaining sections that speak for themselves.
- Passive language and vague responsibility statements.
